⏺ The Keeper's Last Night

  The storm had been building for three days, and Samuel could taste the electricity in the air. At seventy-three, his hands still
  remembered every step of the spiral staircase—one hundred and forty-four stairs to the lantern room. He'd climbed them so many times that
  his body moved by memory alone, leaving his mind free to wander across fifty years of service.

  The lighthouse stood on Mercy Point like a sentinel that would never abandon its post. Samuel had made the same promise to himself the day
  he arrived, fresh from the navy with nothing but a single suitcase and a desperate need to disappear from the world. The keeper's cottage
  had been his sanctuary ever since.

  Tonight would be his last night.

  The automation equipment sat in boxes in his office—state-of-the-art technology that rendered his presence obsolete. The Coast Guard had
  been kind enough to time the switchover for after his birthday. A retirement gift, they'd called it. Samuel called it the end of an era,
  though he'd never say so out loud.

  He reached the lantern room as the first real drops began to fall. The massive Fresnel lens caught the setting sun and fractured it into a
  thousand golden rays. Samuel ran his weathered palm along the brass rail, feeling the salt spray that had accumulated despite the glass
  enclosure. His fingertips had worn grooves into this metal over five decades.

  The beam of light wouldn't activate for another twenty minutes, but Samuel didn't need it to. He knew exactly when the sun would slip
  below the horizon, when the first stars would puncture the darkening sky, and when the lighthouse would earn its keep.

  He thought of Margaret sometimes, though he'd trained himself not to. She'd wanted him to stay on land, to find work in town, to be the
  kind of husband who came home for dinner. But Samuel had carried the ocean in his blood since childhood, and Margaret had eventually
  married the harbor master instead. That had been in 1974. He'd seen their son once at the grocery store in '92—the boy had his mother's
  eyes.

  The storm hit with sudden fury around midnight. Wind screamed against the tower like a living thing trying to tear it apart. Rain hammered
  the gallery windows with such violence that Samuel found himself gripping the rail, though he knew—had always known—that this lighthouse
  was built to endure. Built by men like him who understood that some things needed to stand firm against the chaos.

  He watched the beam sweep across the churning black water, cutting through the rain in rhythmic arcs. Out there, somewhere in that
  darkness, there might be a fishing boat whose captain was grateful for this light. Or perhaps a cargo ship following the coast lane.
  Samuel had never known for certain who benefited from his service. That was the strange luxury of the job—you could do what mattered
  without needing gratitude.

  As the night deepened, the storm intensified. Samuel made his hourly rounds, checking gauges and mechanisms that soon wouldn't need him.
  The log sat open on the desk, waiting for his final entries. Fifty years of nights recorded in careful handwriting: dates, times, weather
  conditions, observations. A chronicle of the invisible work of keeping light burning against the dark.

  Around three in the morning, something shifted. The wind eased slightly, and between the rain squalls, Samuel glimpsed lights on the
  horizon. A ship, moving south along the coast. He tracked it with his eyes as it passed, watching the beam illuminate its path. He'd never
  know who crewed that vessel or where they were headed, but for tonight, his light had guided them safely past the rocks.

  Dawn came slow and grudging, the storm spending its fury as the sky lightened from black to charcoal to grey. Samuel stood in the lantern
  room as the first true light of morning made the lighthouse beam unnecessary. He watched the sun rise over the Atlantic, painting the
  chaos below in shades of purple and gold.

  He would leave after breakfast. The technicians would arrive on Monday to complete the installation. The light would keep
  shining—automated, precise, indifferent. Everything Samuel had been wasn't truly needed anymore.

  But as he descended those one hundred and forty-four steps for the last time, his hand trailing along the rail that held fifty years of
  salt and memory, Samuel realized he didn't feel abandoned. He felt released. His watch was finally over, his vigil complete.

  The light would find another way to burn.
